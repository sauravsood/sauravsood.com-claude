Chores without words

Almost every task app is a list of words. Handmaiden is for homes where the person doing the chores may not read them.

Open tasks.sauravsood.com ↗
Chapter 1

The voice notes

  • The plants get watered on Monday and Thursday, the fridge gets cleaned on the last day of the month, and the brass needs polishing before the festival. In most homes like ours, that schedule lives in someone’s head.

  • It gets passed on in a phone call or a voice note, and by Wednesday the note has scrolled out of sight.

  • The person running the house knows what needs doing and when. The person doing it is often a domestic worker who doesn’t read, or doesn’t read the household’s language, and almost every task app is a list of words.

Chapter 2

The gap

  • Before building anything, Claude and I looked at what exists. Household and to-do apps have good schedules, several users and reminders, but everything in them is written.

  • Picture schedule apps, made for children and people who don’t read, show that a picture and a tick works. They run on one device, though, so nobody can assign a job from elsewhere.

  • Handmaiden joins the two: a detailed admin side for the household, and a worker side made of pictures.

Chapter 3

For the worker

  • Signing in takes no typing. The worker scans a QR code, sees their own face and taps a four-picture code, which works like a PIN made of pictures.

  • Today’s jobs show up as a grid of big photos on a cheap Android phone. Tapping one opens its steps as pictures.

  • When a job is done, the worker taps a big tick. The app asks for a photo as proof only when the task needs one.

  • Time of day is shown as a picture instead of a clock. A small toggle shows the written task, so the worker can hand the phone to someone who reads.

  • The app installs to the home screen and keeps the ticks until the signal comes back.

Chapter 4

For the household

  • The admin side can be as detailed as you like. A task can repeat every few days, weeks, months or years, on chosen weekdays, on the last day of the month, or 10 days after it was last done.

  • A task assigned to three people gives each of them their own copy, so one person’s tick can’t hide someone else’s work.

  • The household can approve, flag, comment with a photo or send a task back. Every attempt stays in the history, the way an issue tracker keeps it.

  • There’s a today board, an overdue list, days off and leave, several admins per home, and on-time rates and streaks that export to CSV.

Chapter 5

About a week

  • 25 Oct: Claude surveyed existing apps and recommended a small custom build that borrows a proven scheduling model.

  • 28 Oct: we settled the rules. Photo proof only when asked, one copy per worker, text behind a toggle and a full review history.

  • 30 Oct: Claude built the database, security rules, schedules, both apps, sign-in, analytics, offline support and tests in one day.

  • 31 Oct: it went live at tasks.sauravsood.com. The first polish fix followed on 2 Nov.

It runs on React and TypeScript on Cloudflare, with Supabase for the database, sign-in, photos and live updates. Each household sees only its own data. The database works out repeating tasks on a timer, and a test throws hundreds of random schedules at both the app and the database to check that they agree.

I decided what mattered for the people using it. Claude did the research, wrote the code and set up the hosting.

Up next

  • The admin will write in their language and the worker will read in theirs, translated automatically.

  • The admin will be able to record a spoken instruction that plays from a speaker button on the photo.
